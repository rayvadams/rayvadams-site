The thermal lens, in pictures

Animations from “The Thermal Lens Revisited,” NSF RTG Conference on Materials and Optimization, University of Utah, October 2026, with K. A. Lurie and W. C. Sanguinet.

The thermal lens is a classic problem of optimal design. Heat enters a square plate along its top edge and leaves through its bottom edge, which is held cold; the sides are insulated. The goal is to send as much of that heat as possible through a window in the middle of the bottom edge. The plate is built from two conductors, a good one, ten times as conductive, and a poor one. Where should the good conductor go?

The answer turns out to need microstructure: fine mixtures of the two materials, laminates, whose layers are aligned with the heat flow. In each animation, J is the heat delivered through the window (the larger the better). For scale: a plate made of either material alone delivers J = 0.5, since the window spans half of the bottom edge and receives half of the heat.

No budget

From a naive funnel to microstructure

Start from the obvious design, a solid funnel of good conductor over the window. It puts good conductor in 75% of the plate and delivers J = 0.9019. Let the computer improve it one cell at a time, and the funnel's walls give way: tongues of both materials grow out of them, ever finer. With pure materials only, the design reaches J = 0.9338 after 270 iterations, using 53% good conductor. On the right is the relaxed optimum, where mixtures are allowed. It delivers more, J = 0.9372, with only 47% good conductor; the tongues are a coarse attempt at that smooth composite. The case for composites is not only the last fraction of a percent in performance. It is cost: the obvious design needs about 60% more of the expensive material and still delivers less.

Pure materials

Pure materials, with a budget

The same experiment at three budgets for the good conductor: unlimited, 20% of the plate, and 10%. With a budget, the design starts as a smooth trumpet over the window. As the mesh is refined (here to 9.2 million cells), the designs grow fronds and J rises, but they stay below the relaxed optimum at every budget. Fine structure is not a numerical artifact; it is what the problem asks for.

1985

1985, revisited

In 1985 Cherkaev, Gibiansky, and Lurie solved the thermal lens with laminates on the computers of the day. Run with today's resources, their scheme never settles on its own: it flips between two states. With damping it settles within about twenty iterations, improving J from 0.9266 to 0.9351. The settled design shows what coarse grids could not: a graded laminate band between the pure zones, in which the mixture follows the Smooth Deployment Law from one pure material to the other. The gold curve is the edge of the composite zone.

1995

A budget for the good conductor

My 1995 paper added a budget: only so much good conductor may be used. Frame by frame, the budget shrinks from unlimited to 3% of the plate, and the price that enforces it rises from 0 to 3.07. Gold marks the edge of the composite; red marks hot spots, where the local heat flux exceeds 1.5 times the mean delivered through the window. The plot on the right shows how evenly the heat arrives across the window. As material grows scarce, the delivery becomes uneven.

2026

A local price for material

The same budgets, now with a price for material that varies across the plate, set by a volume-fraction potential, a term that rewards keeping the material together. Tuned for each budget, it shapes where the material goes and how evenly the heat is delivered; fine texture at the crown of the composite changes J only in the fourth decimal place. At the scarcest budget, no tuning evens out the delivery. That limit is where the current research is headed.

The fine print

Cell-scale texture

Look closely at the raw computed mixtures and you see feathers one to three cells wide at every mesh size, varying the mixture by only 2–3%. Smoothing them out changes J by about one part in ten thousand and leaves the delivery through the window unchanged, so the animations show the mixture averaged over three cells. The large features, the channel's trunk, its taper, and its layering along the heat flow, survive the averaging.
